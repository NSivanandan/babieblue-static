## Clinical Summary: Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)

- **Primary Clinical Focus:**
    Liquid pediatric dosing errors (confusing milligrams [mg] with milliliters [mL], or using household teaspoons instead of calibrated metric oral syringes) account for >70% of preventable emergency department visits for unintentional medication overdoses in children under age 5.

- **Evidence-Based Protocol & Targets:**
    Strictly use metric-only oral syringes (mL) for all infant/toddler liquid medications; dose weight-based Acetaminophen (15 mg/kg every 4–6h, max 5 doses/24h) and Ibuprofen (10 mg/kg every 6–8h ONLY if age >=6 months); and maintain a written shared caregiving dose log.

- **When to Escalate to OB-GYN or Emergency Care:**
    Contact Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US) or emergency care immediately if an infant receives a double dose, adult-strength tablet, Ibuprofen under 6 months of age, or any multi-symptom cold/cough preparation under age 4.

Additionally, providing written materials or resources that patients can refer to at home can further support their understanding and engagement in medication management. ## Technology Solutions for Reducing Medication Errors The advancement of technology has proved to be instrumental in reducing medication errors in healthcare settings. Electronic prescribing (e-prescribing) systems have emerged as a reliable solution to minimize errors in prescription writing and dispensing. These systems allow healthcare providers to electronically transmit prescriptions directly to pharmacies, eliminating the possibility of misinterpretation or illegible handwriting. By seamlessly integrating patient information, drug databases, and clinical decision support tools, e-prescribing systems enhance accuracy, streamline workflows, and ultimately improve patient safety. Additionally, barcode technology has gained popularity in medication administration processes. By scanning barcode labels on medication packages and patient identification wristbands, healthcare professionals can verify that the right medication is being administered to the right patient, reducing the risk of medication errors and adverse effects. Information technology also plays a vital role in medication reconciliation, a process aimed at ensuring the accuracy and consistency of medication information during transitions of care. Electronic health records (EHRs) enable healthcare providers to access comprehensive medication histories, including prescribed medications, over-the-counter drugs, and supplements, thus facilitating accurate reconciliation. Integration of clinical decision support systems within EHRs further aids in identifying potential medication errors, offering real-time alerts and recommendations to healthcare professionals. Technology solutions thus offer promising avenues for reducing medication errors, improving patient safety, and enhancing the overall quality of healthcare delivery. ## Improving Communication and Collaboration in Medication Administration Effective communication and collaboration are essential components in ensuring safe and accurate medication administration. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends against routinely alternating Acetaminophen and Ibuprofen because fever itself is a protective immune response (not an illness), and alternating schedules drastically increase the risk of caregiver confusion and accidental Acetaminophen or Ibuprofen overdose at night. Use a single agent focused on the child's comfort, and only transition or combine under specific guidance from your pediatrician with a written timestamp chart. What should I do if my baby spits up or vomits right after I give liquid medicine? It depends on the exact timing and medication class. If the child vomits a visible puddle immediately (within 5–10 minutes) of an antibiotic or antipyretic, contact your pharmacist or pediatrician before repeating the dose. If more than 20–30 minutes have elapsed since swallowing, the majority of liquid medication has already been absorbed across the gastric mucosa and repeating the dose risks an overdose. Aiming the oral syringe slowly toward the back inside pocket of the cheek (buccal pouch) prevents gagging and spitting. Why are prenatal vitamins one of the most dangerous household poisoning hazards for toddlers? Adult prenatal vitamins contain high concentrations of elemental iron (typically 27 mg to 65 mg per tablet) and are often candy-coated or formulated as gummies. In a 10–12 kg toddler, ingesting just 10–15 prenatal iron tablets (>20–60 mg/kg of elemental iron) causes severe corrosive hemorrhagic gastritis, metabolic acidosis, hepatic necrosis, and cardiovascular shock. Always treat prenatal iron vitamins with the same locked-cabinet security as prescription medications. Q1: What are medication errors, and how do they occur? A1: Medication errors are preventable mistakes in the process of prescribing, dispensing, administering, or monitoring medications that lead to patient harm. They can occur due to various factors, including miscommunication, similar drug names, look-alike packaging, inadequate training, distractions, and system failures. Errors may involve the wrong medication, dosage, route, or patient. Q2: What are the common causes of medication errors? A2: Common causes of medication errors include communication breakdowns, similar-sounding medications, look-alike packaging, inadequate training of healthcare professionals, and distractions in the workplace. High-risk medications, patient-related factors like language barriers and health literacy, and problems within systems and processes also contribute to errors.
